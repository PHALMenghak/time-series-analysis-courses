Executive Summary

In quantitative finance and empirical asset pricing, modeling financial returns is foundational to risk management, algorithmic execution, and derivatives pricing. This case study applies the Box–Jenkins modeling strategy to daily closing prices from the benchmark EuStockMarkets dataset in R (1991–1998, \(N = 1,860\) daily observations per index), focusing on the FTSE 100 index.

We demonstrate the transition from non-stationary asset price levels (\(I(1)\)) to stationary continuously compounded log returns (\(I(0)\)). We rigorously evaluate the Weak-Form Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) and show that while daily index returns display statistically detectable first-order autoregressive persistence (\(\text{AR}(1)\) with \(\hat{\phi}_1 = 0.0678, p = 0.0066\)) due to market microstructure and non-synchronous trading, the economic magnitude is modest. Furthermore, residual diagnostics demonstrate the textbook stylized facts of financial time series: absence of linear serial correlation alongside pronounced volatility clustering and leptokurtosis, directly motivating the transition to ARCH/GARCH models.

1 Financial Econometric Foundations

1.1 The Random Walk and Martingale Difference Hypotheses

Let \(P_t\) denote the closing price of an asset at trading day \(t\). Under the classical Random Walk Hypothesis (Fama, 1970), price increments are independent and identically distributed:

\[ \ln(P_t) = \mu + \ln(P_{t-1}) + e_t, \qquad e_t \stackrel{i.i.d.}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2). \]

A more general, economically grounded formulation is the Martingale Difference Hypothesis (MDH), which states that the conditional expectation of future price innovations given all publicly available market information \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) is zero:

\[ E\bigl[P_t - P_{t-1} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\bigr] = 0 \iff E\bigl[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\bigr] = \mu, \]

where \(r_t = 100 \times \ln(P_t / P_{t-1})\) represents the percentage log return.

1.2 Why Index Returns Exhibit Weak Autocorrelation: Non-Synchronous Trading

If individual asset returns are martingales, why do composite market indices (such as the FTSE 100 or S&P 500) frequently display positive first-order autocorrelation (\(\phi_1 > 0\))?

As established by Lo and MacKinlay (1990) and Campbell, Lo, and MacKinlay (1997), broad market indices suffer from non-synchronous trading (or stale pricing): 1. Heavily traded, large-capitalization stocks incorporate market-wide macroeconomic shocks almost instantly at the end of the trading day. 2. Smaller, less liquid constituents of the index trade less frequently, and their recorded closing prices reflect earlier daytime information. 3. Consequently, information that moved large-cap stocks today only gets reflected in small-cap closing prices tomorrow, inducing a spurious positive lag-1 autocorrelation in the aggregate index:

\[ r_t^{\text{index}} = \mu + \phi_1 r_{t-1}^{\text{index}} + e_t, \qquad \phi_1 > 0. \]

2 Step 1: Data Preparation and Financial Returns Construction

2.1 Purpose

To inspect the multidimensional structure of the EuStockMarkets dataset, compute continuously compounded log returns, and partition the data into in-sample estimation and out-of-sample forecast evaluation windows.

2.2 Statistical Concept

Financial asset prices \(P_t\) are strictly positive and typically non-stationary. Econometric analysis is performed on continuously compounded log returns:

\[ r_t = 100 \times \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right) = 100 \times \bigl[\ln(P_t) - \ln(P_{t-1})\bigr], \]

which possess convenient mathematical properties: 1. Time Additivity: Multi-period log return over \(k\) days equals the sum of single-day returns: \(r_{t, k} = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} r_{t-i}\). 2. Stationarity: Log-differencing eliminates stochastic trends, yielding a mean-reverting series.

3 Step 2: Exploratory Data Analysis & Financial Time Series Dynamics

3.1 Purpose

To visually compare non-stationary price dynamics with mean-reverting daily returns and identify volatility clustering.

3.2 Statistical Concept

A stationary process fluctuates around a constant mean with constant variance. While daily returns fluctuate around an unconditional mean near zero, the magnitude of the fluctuations changes over time—a phenomenon known as Mandelbrot’s volatility clustering (1963): large returns (of either sign) cluster together in high-volatility regimes.

4 Step 3: Unit Root and Stationarity Testing

4.1 Purpose

To verify through formal econometric testing that index prices are non-stationary (\(I(1)\)) and returns are stationary (\(I(0)\)).

4.2 Statistical Concept

The Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) regression tests for the existence of a unit root:

\[ \Delta y_t = c + \beta t + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^k \psi_i \Delta y_{t-i} + e_t, \]

under \(H_0: \gamma = 0\) (unit root) versus \(H_1: \gamma < 0\) (stationary).

4.3 R Implementation

“To confirm the integration order of the series, Augmented Dickey–Fuller tests were conducted on both price levels and continuously compounded returns. In contrast, the ADF test on daily log returns strongly rejects the unit root hypothesis (\(DF = -10.985, p < 0.01\)). Hence, the return series is stationary in levels (\(I(0)\)), justifying linear autoregressive modeling without further differencing.”

5 Step 4: Model Identification via ACF and PACF

5.1 Purpose

To identify candidate autoregressive orders \(p\) by inspecting the empirical autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation functions.

5.2 Statistical Concept

Under the Efficient Market Hypothesis, returns should behave as white noise with all autocorrelations \(\rho_k = 0\) for \(k \geq 1\). Under non-synchronous trading, a small but statistically significant spike occurs at lag 1 in the PACF (\(\phi_{11} > 0\)), while all higher-order partial autocorrelations vanish.

The 95% Bartlett confidence envelope for sample size \(n = 1,600\) is:

\[ \text{CI}_{0.95} = \pm \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{1600}} = \pm \frac{1.96}{40} = \pm 0.0490. \]

# Function to extract coefficients and p-values
extract_ar_table <- function(m, p, name) {
  if (p == 0) {
    coefs <- m$coef["intercept"]
    ses   <- sqrt(m$var.coef["intercept", "intercept"])
  } else {
    coefs <- c(m$coef[1:p], m$coef["intercept"])
    ses   <- sqrt(diag(m$var.coef))[c(1:p, length(m$coef))]
  }
  t_val <- coefs / ses
  p_val <- 2 * (1 - pnorm(abs(t_val)))
  
  data.frame(
    Model     = name,
    Parameter = names(coefs),
    Estimate  = round(coefs, 4),
    SE        = round(ses, 4),
    t_stat    = round(t_val, 3),
    p_value   = format.pval(p_val, eps = 0.001, digits = 3)
  )
}

  extract_ar_table(m1, 1, "AR(1)"),
  extract_ar_table(m2, 2, "AR(2)"),
  extract_ar_table(m3, 3, "AR(3)")

7.3 The Crucial Financial Insight: Statistical vs Economic Significance

ImportantWhy an \(\text{AR}(1)\) Model Cannot Beat the Market
  1. Statistical Significance (\(p = 0.0066\)): The large sample size (\(n = 1,600\)) provides high statistical power, detecting small deviations from zero (\(R^2 = \phi_1^2 \approx 0.0678^2 \approx 0.46\%\)).
  2. Economic Insignificance: The proportion of variance explained by the model is less than half of one percent (\(0.46\%\)). A \(1\%\) return yesterday predicts only a \(0.068\%\) return today.
  3. Transaction Costs: Typical bid-ask spreads, broker commissions, and execution slippage in equity index futures during the 1990s were \(0.10\%\) to \(0.20\%\) per round-trip trade—far exceeding the \(0.068\%\) predicted move.
  4. Hence, the \(\text{AR}(1)\) model is consistent with the Weak-Form Efficient Market Hypothesis after accounting for institutional transaction costs.

8 Step 7: Model Diagnostics & Assessment of Stylized Facts

8.1 Purpose

To test whether residuals satisfy white noise conditions and explore non-linear volatility clustering.

8.2 Statistical Concept

  1. Linear Autocorrelation: Tested using the Ljung–Box Portmanteau test with adjusted degrees of freedom \(df = K - p\).
  2. Normality & Fat Tails: Evaluated via Q–Q plot and Shapiro–Wilk test.
  3. Non-Linear Dependence (ARCH Effect): Evaluated by testing for autocorrelation in squared residuals \(\hat{e}_t^2\). Even when \(\operatorname{Cov}(e_t, e_{t-k}) = 0\), if \(\operatorname{Cov}(e_t^2, e_{t-k}^2) \neq 0\), the process exhibits conditional heteroscedasticity.

10 Step 9: Financial Engineering Discussion, Limitations & Defense Preparation

10.1 Key Takeaways

  1. Market Microstructure Autocorrelation: The FTSE 100 exhibits statistically significant positive first-order autocorrelation (\(\hat{\phi}_1 = 0.0678, p = 0.0066\)) due to non-synchronous trading of composite index constituents.
  2. Economic vs Statistical Significance: An \(R^2\) of \(0.46\%\) cannot overcome execution costs, preserving the validity of the Weak-Form Efficient Market Hypothesis.
  3. The Limits of Linear AR Models: While the \(\text{AR}(1)\) filters out all linear dependence in returns, its residuals violate the constant variance assumption. The presence of significant autocorrelation in squared residuals (\(\hat{e}_t^2\)) demonstrates the necessity of conditional heteroscedasticity models (ARCH/GARCH).

10.2 Anticipated Academic Defense Questions & Model Answers

ImportantQuestion 1: If markets are efficient, why did you find a statistically significant AR(1) coefficient?

Model Answer: In broad market capitalization indices such as the FTSE 100, index constituents do not trade at identical frequencies. As proven by Lo and MacKinlay (1990), non-synchronous trading causes information absorbed by liquid large-caps to appear with a one-day lag in less liquid small-caps, creating spurious positive index autocorrelation. However, the \(R^2\) is only \(0.46\%\), which is smaller than typical transaction costs (\(0.10\text{--}0.20\%\)). Therefore, the market is efficient in an economic sense.

ImportantQuestion 2: Why do linear residuals pass the Ljung–Box test while squared residuals fail?

Model Answer: The linear Ljung–Box test evaluates whether \(\operatorname{Cov}(e_t, e_{t-k}) = 0\). Because returns are linearly uncorrelated, the linear test passes (\(p = 0.308\)). However, independence requires \(\operatorname{Cov}(g(e_t), h(e_{t-k})) = 0\) for all functions \(g\) and \(h\). The squared residuals \(\hat{e}_t^2\) capture volatility magnitude. Because financial volatility clusters over time, \(\operatorname{Cov}(e_t^2, e_{t-k}^2) \neq 0\), causing the squared test to fail (\(p < 0.0001\)) and revealing the ARCH effect.

ImportantQuestion 3: Why does the AR(1) forecast flatline after just two days?

Model Answer: The speed of mean reversion in an \(\text{AR}(1)\) process is governed by the magnitude of \(|\phi_1|\). Since \(\hat{\phi}_1 = 0.0678\), the forecast at horizon \(h\) is \(\hat{r}_{n+h} = \mu + \phi_1^h (r_n - \mu)\). For \(h = 2\), \(\phi_1^2 = 0.0046 \approx 0\), meaning \(99.5\%\) of any initial return shock is dissipated within 48 trading hours, converging to the unconditional drift \(\mu\).

In quantitative finance and empirical asset pricing, modeling financial returns is foundational to risk management, algorithmic execution, and derivatives pricing. ## Financial Econometric Foundations

### The Random Walk and Martingale Difference Hypotheses

Let $P_t$ denote the closing price of an asset at trading day $t$. Under the classical Random Walk Hypothesis (Fama, 1970), price increments are independent and identically distributed:

$$
\ln(P_t) = \mu + \ln(P_{t-1}) + e_t, \qquad e_t \stackrel{i.i.d.}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2).
$$

A more general, economically grounded formulation is the **Martingale Difference Hypothesis (MDH)**, which states that the conditional expectation of future price innovations given all publicly available market information $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ is zero:

$$
E\bigl[P_t - P_{t-1} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\bigr] = 0 \iff E\bigl[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\bigr] = \mu,
$$

where $r_t = 100 \times \ln(P_t / P_{t-1})$ represents the percentage log return.

### Why Index Returns Exhibit Weak Autocorrelation: Non-Synchronous Trading

If individual asset returns are martingales, why do composite market indices (such as the FTSE 100 or S&P 500) frequently display positive first-order autocorrelation ($\phi_1 > 0$)?

As established by **Lo and MacKinlay (1990)** and **Campbell, Lo, and MacKinlay (1997)**, broad market indices suffer from **non-synchronous trading** (or stale pricing):
1. Heavily traded, large-capitalization stocks incorporate market-wide macroeconomic shocks almost instantly at the end of the trading day.
2. Smaller, less liquid constituents of the index trade less frequently, and their recorded closing prices reflect earlier daytime information.
3. Consequently, information that moved large-cap stocks today only gets reflected in small-cap closing prices tomorrow, inducing a spurious positive lag-1 autocorrelation in the aggregate index:

$$
r_t^{\text{index}} = \mu + \phi_1 r_{t-1}^{\text{index}} + e_t, \qquad \phi_1 > 0.
$$

### Statistical Concept

$$

1. **Time Additivity:** Multi-period log return over $k$ days equals the sum of single-day returns: $r_{t, k} = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} r_{t-i}$.

## Step 3: Unit Root and Stationarity Testing

### Purpose
To verify through formal econometric testing that index prices are non-stationary ($I(1)$) and returns are stationary ($I(0)$).

### Statistical Concept
The **Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF)** regression tests for the existence of a unit root:

$$
\Delta y_t = c + \beta t + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^k \psi_i \Delta y_{t-i} + e_t,
$$

under $H_0: \gamma = 0$ (unit root) versus $H_1: \gamma < 0$ (stationary).

### R Implementation

extract_ar_table <- function(m, p, name) {
  if (p == 0) {
    coefs <- m$coef["intercept"]
    ses   <- sqrt(m$var.coef["intercept", "intercept"])
  } else {
    coefs <- c(m$coef[1:p], m$coef["intercept"])
    ses   <- sqrt(diag(m$var.coef))[c(1:p, length(m$coef))]
  }
  t_val <- coefs / ses
  p_val <- 2 * (1 - pnorm(abs(t_val)))
  
  data.frame(
    Model     = name,
    Parameter = names(coefs),
    Estimate  = round(coefs, 4),
    SE        = round(ses, 4),
    t_stat    = round(t_val, 3),
    p_value   = format.pval(p_val, eps = 0.001, digits = 3)
  )
}

param_summary <- rbind(
  extract_ar_table(m0, 0, "AR(0) / WN"),
  extract_ar_table(m1, 1, "AR(1)"),
  extract_ar_table(m2, 2, "AR(2)"),
  extract_ar_table(m3, 3, "AR(3)")
)

kable(param_summary, row.names = FALSE,
      caption = "Parameter Estimates, Standard Errors, and Significance Tests for Candidate AR Models")

## Step 7: Model Diagnostics & Assessment of Stylized Facts

### Purpose
To test whether residuals satisfy white noise conditions and explore non-linear volatility clustering.

### Statistical Concept
1. **Linear Autocorrelation:** Tested using the Ljung–Box Portmanteau test with adjusted degrees of freedom $df = K - p$.
2. **Normality & Fat Tails:** Evaluated via Q–Q plot and Shapiro–Wilk test.
3. **Non-Linear Dependence (ARCH Effect):** Evaluated by testing for autocorrelation in **squared residuals** $\hat{e}_t^2$. Even when $\operatorname{Cov}(e_t, e_{t-k}) = 0$, if $\operatorname{Cov}(e_t^2, e_{t-k}^2) \neq 0$, the process exhibits **conditional heteroscedasticity**.

## Step 9: Financial Engineering Discussion, Limitations & Defense Preparation

### Key Takeaways

1. **Market Microstructure Autocorrelation:** The FTSE 100 exhibits statistically significant positive first-order autocorrelation ($\hat{\phi}_1 = 0.0678, p = 0.0066$) due to non-synchronous trading of composite index constituents.
2. **Economic vs Statistical Significance:** An $R^2$ of $0.46\%$ cannot overcome execution costs, preserving the validity of the Weak-Form Efficient Market Hypothesis.
3. **The Limits of Linear AR Models:** While the $\text{AR}(1)$ filters out all linear dependence in returns, its residuals violate the constant variance assumption. The presence of significant autocorrelation in squared residuals ($\hat{e}_t^2$) demonstrates the necessity of conditional heteroscedasticity models (ARCH/GARCH).

### Anticipated Academic Defense Questions & Model Answers

::: {.callout-important}
### Question 1: If markets are efficient, why did you find a statistically significant AR(1) coefficient?
**Model Answer:** In broad market capitalization indices such as the FTSE 100, index constituents do not trade at identical frequencies. As proven by Lo and MacKinlay (1990), non-synchronous trading causes information absorbed by liquid large-caps to appear with a one-day lag in less liquid small-caps, creating spurious positive index autocorrelation. However, the $R^2$ is only $0.46\%$, which is smaller than typical transaction costs ($0.10\text{--}0.20\%$). Therefore, the market is efficient in an economic sense.
:::

::: {.callout-important}
### Question 2: Why do linear residuals pass the Ljung–Box test while squared residuals fail?
**Model Answer:** The linear Ljung–Box test evaluates whether $\operatorname{Cov}(e_t, e_{t-k}) = 0$. Because returns are linearly uncorrelated, the linear test passes ($p = 0.308$). However, independence requires $\operatorname{Cov}(g(e_t), h(e_{t-k})) = 0$ for all functions $g$ and $h$. The squared residuals $\hat{e}_t^2$ capture volatility magnitude. Because financial volatility clusters over time, $\operatorname{Cov}(e_t^2, e_{t-k}^2) \neq 0$, causing the squared test to fail ($p < 0.0001$) and revealing the ARCH effect.
:::

::: {.callout-important}
### Question 3: Why does the AR(1) forecast flatline after just two days?
**Model Answer:** The speed of mean reversion in an $\text{AR}(1)$ process is governed by the magnitude of $|\phi_1|$. Since $\hat{\phi}_1 = 0.0678$, the forecast at horizon $h$ is $\hat{r}_{n+h} = \mu + \phi_1^h (r_n - \mu)$. For $h = 2$, $\phi_1^2 = 0.0046 \approx 0$, meaning $99.5\%$ of any initial return shock is dissipated within 48 trading hours, converging to the unconditional drift $\mu$.
:::
