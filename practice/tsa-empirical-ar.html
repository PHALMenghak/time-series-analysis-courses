Executive Summary

This empirical study provides an end-to-end, reproducible implementation of the Autoregressive (\(AR(p)\)) model within the classic Box–Jenkins framework. Using the historical annual Canadian Lynx trappings series (1821–1934, \(n = 114\)), we systematically execute data integrity validation, exploratory data analysis, stationarity testing, model identification via ACF/PACF, maximum likelihood parameter estimation, multi-criteria model selection, residual diagnostic checking, and out-of-sample dynamic forecasting with prediction intervals.

All computations are executed natively in R with zero fabricated metrics. The workflow illustrates how to defend modeling decisions with statistical rigor, explaining why parsimonious criteria (such as BIC) and out-of-sample predictive accuracy prevent in-sample overfitting.

1 Theoretical Framework of the \(AR(p)\) Model

An Autoregressive process of order \(p\), denoted \(\text{AR}(p)\), models the current observation \(Y_t\) as a linear combination of its \(p\) most recent past values plus an exogenous stochastic shock:

\[ Y_t = c + \phi_1 Y_{t-1} + \phi_2 Y_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_p Y_{t-p} + e_t, \]

where:

  • \(c = \mu(1 - \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i)\) is the constant term related to the unconditional process mean \(\mu = E(Y_t)\).
  • \(\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots, \phi_p\) are the autoregressive coefficients.
  • \(\{e_t\} \stackrel{i.i.d.}{\sim} \mathcal{W}\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_e^2)\) is a Gaussian white noise innovation sequence with \(E(e_t) = 0\), \(\operatorname{Var}(e_t) = \sigma_e^2\), and \(\operatorname{Cov}(e_t, e_s) = 0\) for \(t \neq s\).

Using the backshift (lag) operator \(B\), where \(B^k Y_t = Y_{t-k}\), the model is concisely expressed as:

\[ \Phi(B) Y_t = c + e_t, \qquad \text{where } \Phi(B) = 1 - \phi_1 B - \phi_2 B^2 - \dots - \phi_p B^p. \]

1.1 Stationarity and Invertibility Conditions

An \(\text{AR}(p)\) process is weakly (covariance) stationary if and only if all roots of the characteristic equation

\[ \Phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \phi_2 z^2 - \dots - \phi_p z^p = 0 \]

lie strictly outside the complex unit circle, that is, \(|z_j| > 1\) for all \(j = 1, \dots, p\).

For an \(\text{AR}(1)\) process, this reduces to \(|\phi_1| < 1\). For an \(\text{AR}(2)\) process, the stationarity region forms a triangular parameter space:

\[ \phi_1 + \phi_2 < 1, \qquad \phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1, \qquad |\phi_2| < 1. \]

When \(\phi_1^2 + 4\phi_2 < 0\), the roots of the characteristic equation form a complex conjugate pair \(z_{1,2} = r e^{\pm i \theta}\), generating damped quasi-cyclical fluctuations with characteristic frequency \(\theta\) and average cycle length:

\[ \theta = \arccos\left(\frac{\phi_1}{2\sqrt{-\phi_2}}\right), \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\theta}. \]

1.2 Theoretical Signatures of ACF and PACF

Function \(\text{AR}(p)\) Behavior \(\text{MA}(q)\) Behavior \(\text{ARMA}(p, q)\) Behavior
ACF (\(\rho_k\)) Tails off (exponential decay or damped sine waves) Cuts off after lag \(q\) Tails off after lag \(q - p\)
PACF (\(\phi_{kk}\)) Cuts off abruptly after lag \(p\) (\(\phi_{kk} = 0, \forall k > p\)) Tails off (exponential decay or damped sine waves) Tails off after lag \(p - q\)

This fundamental duality makes the Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF) the primary diagnostic tool for identifying the order \(p\) of an autoregressive process.

2 Step 1: Data Preparation and Integrity Check

2.1 Purpose

To inspect the structural properties of the raw dataset, confirm the sampling interval, test for missing values and duplicates, and compute standard descriptive statistics.

2.2 Statistical Concept

A time series must possess a consistent sampling grid without irregular timestamp gaps or corrupted values before applying linear time-invariant filters. Extreme skewness and heavy tails frequently violate Gaussian innovation assumptions, requiring variance-stabilizing non-linear transformations (such as Box–Cox or logarithmic transformations).

4 Step 3: Stationarity Assessment and Differencing Decision

4.1 Purpose

To formally test whether the series possesses a unit root (stochastic trend) or is stationary around a constant mean.

4.2 Statistical Concept

The Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test evaluates the null hypothesis of a unit root (\(H_0: \gamma = 0\)) against the alternative of covariance stationarity (\(H_1: \gamma < 0\)) in the test regression:

\[ \Delta Y_t = c + \beta t + \gamma Y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \psi_i \Delta Y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t. \]

If \(H_0\) is rejected (\(p < 0.05\)), the process is stationary in levels (\(I(0)\)). Differencing an already stationary process (\(\nabla Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}\)) is known as overdifferencing; it injects an artificial non-invertible moving average unit root (\(\theta = 1\)) into the innovation structure, inflating parameter variances and distorting long-term forecasts.

5 Step 4: Model Identification via ACF and PACF

5.1 Purpose

To analyze empirical autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation functions to determine candidate autoregressive orders (\(p\)).

5.2 Statistical Concept

For an \(\text{AR}(p)\) process: 1. Sample Autocorrelation Function (ACF): Satisfies the Yule–Walker difference equations, generating damped exponential decay or damped sinusoidal waves. 2. Sample Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF): Measures the correlation between \(Y_t\) and \(Y_{t-k}\) after filtering out the linear dependence explained by intermediate lags \(Y_{t-1}, \dots, Y_{t-k+1}\). Theoretical PACF cuts off sharply to zero for all lags \(k > p\):

\[ \phi_{kk} = 0 \quad \text{for all } k > p. \]

Under the null hypothesis of an \(\text{AR}(p)\) process, sample PACF values beyond lag \(p\) are asymptotically normal with variance \(1/n\), yielding Bartlett 95% confidence intervals at:

\[ \text{CI}_{0.95} = \pm \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{n}}. \]

6 Step 5: Model Estimation and Parameter Significance

6.1 Purpose

To estimate the parameters of the candidate models using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE), extract standard errors, test coefficient significance, and evaluate characteristic roots.

6.2 Statistical Concept

Parameters are estimated via Maximum Likelihood Estimation initialized through the Kalman filter. The asymptotic covariance matrix of the estimators \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\) is obtained from the inverse Fisher Information matrix:

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}) = \mathcal{I}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}). \]

For each coefficient, we evaluate the Wald statistic:

\[ t = \frac{\hat{\phi}_j}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\phi}_j)} \stackrel{a}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1). \]

Coefficients are statistically significant at \(\alpha = 0.05\) if \(|t| > 1.96\) (\(p < 0.05\)).

7 Step 6: Multi-Criteria Model Selection

7.1 Purpose

To compare candidate models across goodness-of-fit, parameter penalty criteria, and residual variance, explaining why model choice cannot rely on AIC alone.

7.2 Statistical Concept

Goodness-of-fit naturally improves as additional lags are appended to an autoregressive model. Penalized likelihood criteria balance in-sample fit against model complexity:

\[ \text{AIC} = -2\ln(\hat{L}) + 2k, \]

\[ \text{BIC} = -2\ln(\hat{L}) + k\ln(n), \]

where \(k\) is the total number of estimated parameters (including autoregressive parameters, constant, and innovation variance \(\sigma_e^2\)), and \(n\) is the training sample size (\(n = 100\)).

7.3 Why Model Selection Cannot Rely on AIC Alone

NoteCritical Distinction Between AIC and BIC
  • AIC is asymptotically efficient; its penalty (\(2k\)) reflects the Kullback–Leibler information discrepancy. However, in small-to-moderate samples, AIC has a non-zero probability of over-parameterizing autoregressive processes because the penalty does not grow with sample size.
  • BIC is dimensionally consistent; because \(\ln(n) = \ln(100) \approx 4.605 > 2\), BIC penalizes additional coefficients more than twice as heavily as AIC. Under the true finite \(\text{AR}(p)\) model, the probability that BIC selects the correct order approaches 1 as \(n \to \infty\).
  • Relying on AIC alone risks selecting overfitted specifications that capture in-sample noise at the expense of out-of-sample forecast accuracy.

7.4 R Implementation

Code

8 Step 7: Model Diagnostics and Residual Verification

8.1 Purpose

To rigorously check that the residuals of the fitted \(\text{AR}(2)\) model satisfy white noise assumptions: zero mean, homoscedasticity, absence of serial correlation, and approximate Gaussianity.

8.2 Statistical Concept

Let \(\hat{e}_t = Y_t - \hat{Y}_t\) be the model residuals. 1. Ljung–Box Portmanteau Test: Evaluates whether residual autocorrelations up to lag \(K\) are jointly zero: \[ Q^*(K) = n(n + 2) \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\hat{r}_k^2(\hat{e})}{n - k} \stackrel{H_0}{\sim} \chi^2(K - p), \] where degrees of freedom must be adjusted by subtracting the \(p = 2\) estimated AR coefficients (\(df = K - p\)). 2. Normality: Assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test and Normal Quantile–Quantile (Q–Q) plots.

9 Step 8: Out-of-Sample Forecasting and Empirical Evaluation

9.1 Purpose

To generate multi-step dynamic out-of-sample forecasts over the 14-year post-sample horizon (1921–1934), compute prediction intervals, and evaluate forecast accuracy against the competing specifications.

9.2 Statistical Concept

For an \(\text{AR}(2)\) process, point forecasts \(\hat{Y}_{n+h|n}\) are computed recursively:

\[ \hat{Y}_{n+1|n} = c + \phi_1 Y_n + \phi_2 Y_{n-1}, \]

\[ \hat{Y}_{n+h|n} = c + \phi_1 \hat{Y}_{n+h-1|n} + \phi_2 \hat{Y}_{n+h-2|n} \quad (h \geq 2). \]

The \(100(1 - \alpha)\%\) prediction interval at horizon \(h\) is given by:

\[ \hat{Y}_{n+h|n} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sigma_e \sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{h-1} \psi_j^2}, \]

where \(\psi_j\) are the impulse-response coefficients from the Wold representation \(Y_t = \mu + \sum_{j=0}^\infty \psi_j e_{t-j}\).

Out-of-sample performance is quantified on the reserved test partition (\(h = 14\)) using:

  • Mean Absolute Error (MAE): \(\frac{1}{h}\sum_{i=1}^h |Y_{n+i} - \hat{Y}_{n+i|n}|\)
  • Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): \(\sqrt{\frac{1}{h}\sum_{i=1}^h (Y_{n+i} - \hat{Y}_{n+i|n})^2}\)
  • Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE): \(\frac{100\%}{h}\sum_{i=1}^h \left|\frac{Y_{n+i} - \hat{Y}_{n+i|n}}{Y_{n+i}}\right|\)

10.3 Anticipated Academic Defense Questions & Model Answers

ImportantQuestion 1: Why did you not difference the series before fitting an AR model?

Model Answer: The Augmented Dickey–Fuller test on the training partition yielded \(\text{DF} = -4.6713\) with \(p < 0.01\), firmly rejecting the null hypothesis of a unit root. Differencing a series that is already stationary (\(I(0)\)) constitutes overdifferencing. Overdifferencing introduces an artificial moving average unit root (\(\theta = 1\)), increases residual variance, and degrades long-term forecasts.

ImportantQuestion 2: AR(4) achieved a lower AIC than AR(2). Why did you select AR(2)?

Model Answer: AIC penalizes complexity by \(2k\), which has a well-known asymptotic probability of selecting over-parameterized models in finite samples. BIC uses \(k\ln(n) \approx 4.605k\), which penalizes redundant parameters more severely and is consistent. In our data, the 3rd and 4th lag coefficients were statistically insignificant (\(p > 0.05\)). Furthermore, out-of-sample evaluation demonstrated that \(\text{AR}(2)\) achieved lower MAE and RMSE than \(\text{AR}(4)\), confirming that the lower AIC of \(\text{AR}(4)\) was the result of in-sample overfitting.

ImportantQuestion 3: How do you justify the degrees of freedom used in the Ljung–Box test?

Model Answer: When testing the residuals of an estimated \(\text{AR}(p)\) model, the test statistic does not follow a \(\chi^2(K)\) distribution because the residuals are estimated from the data, which consumes degrees of freedom. Box and Pierce (1970) and Ljung and Box (1978) established that the null distribution is asymptotically \(\chi^2(K - p)\). Failing to subtract the \(p = 2\) estimated parameters would lead to overly conservative test statistics and inflated \(p\)-values.

## Theoretical Framework of the $AR(p)$ Model

An Autoregressive process of order $p$, denoted $\text{AR}(p)$, models the current observation $Y_t$ as a linear combination of its $p$ most recent past values plus an exogenous stochastic shock:

$$
Y_t = c + \phi_1 Y_{t-1} + \phi_2 Y_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_p Y_{t-p} + e_t,
$$

where:

- $c = \mu(1 - \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i)$ is the constant term related to the unconditional process mean $\mu = E(Y_t)$.
- $\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots, \phi_p$ are the autoregressive coefficients.
- $\{e_t\} \stackrel{i.i.d.}{\sim} \mathcal{W}\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_e^2)$ is a Gaussian white noise innovation sequence with $E(e_t) = 0$, $\operatorname{Var}(e_t) = \sigma_e^2$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(e_t, e_s) = 0$ for $t \neq s$.

Using the backshift (lag) operator $B$, where $B^k Y_t = Y_{t-k}$, the model is concisely expressed as:

$$
\Phi(B) Y_t = c + e_t, \qquad \text{where } \Phi(B) = 1 - \phi_1 B - \phi_2 B^2 - \dots - \phi_p B^p.
$$

### Stationarity and Invertibility Conditions

An $\text{AR}(p)$ process is weakly (covariance) stationary if and only if all roots of the characteristic equation

$$
\Phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \phi_2 z^2 - \dots - \phi_p z^p = 0
$$

lie strictly **outside the complex unit circle**, that is, $|z_j| > 1$ for all $j = 1, \dots, p$. 

For an $\text{AR}(1)$ process, this reduces to $|\phi_1| < 1$. For an $\text{AR}(2)$ process, the stationarity region forms a triangular parameter space:

$$
\phi_1 + \phi_2 < 1, \qquad \phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1, \qquad |\phi_2| < 1.
$$

When $\phi_1^2 + 4\phi_2 < 0$, the roots of the characteristic equation form a complex conjugate pair $z_{1,2} = r e^{\pm i \theta}$, generating damped quasi-cyclical fluctuations with characteristic frequency $\theta$ and average cycle length:

$$
\theta = \arccos\left(\frac{\phi_1}{2\sqrt{-\phi_2}}\right), \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\theta}.
$$

### Theoretical Signatures of ACF and PACF

| Function | $\text{AR}(p)$ Behavior | $\text{MA}(q)$ Behavior | $\text{ARMA}(p, q)$ Behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ACF ($\rho_k$)** | Tails off (exponential decay or damped sine waves) | Cuts off after lag $q$ | Tails off after lag $q - p$ |
| **PACF ($\phi_{kk}$)** | **Cuts off abruptly after lag $p$** ($\phi_{kk} = 0, \forall k > p$) | Tails off (exponential decay or damped sine waves) | Tails off after lag $p - q$ |

This fundamental duality makes the **Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF)** the primary diagnostic tool for identifying the order $p$ of an autoregressive process.

## Step 3: Stationarity Assessment and Differencing Decision

### Purpose
To formally test whether the series possesses a unit root (stochastic trend) or is stationary around a constant mean.

### Statistical Concept
The **Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF)** test evaluates the null hypothesis of a unit root ($H_0: \gamma = 0$) against the alternative of covariance stationarity ($H_1: \gamma < 0$) in the test regression:

$$
\Delta Y_t = c + \beta t + \gamma Y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \psi_i \Delta Y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t.
$$

If $H_0$ is rejected ($p < 0.05$), the process is stationary in levels ($I(0)$). Differencing an already stationary process ($\nabla Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}$) is known as **overdifferencing**; it injects an artificial non-invertible moving average unit root ($\theta = 1$) into the innovation structure, inflating parameter variances and distorting long-term forecasts.

### R Implementation

```{r}

                      "Reject H0: Stationary (I(0))",

kable(adf_summary, caption = "Formal Unit Root Assessment on Training Partition")
```

### Output Interpretation
- The Augmented Dickey–Fuller test on the training partition yields a test statistic of $\text{DF} = -4.6713$ with an empirical $p$-value bounded at $p = 0.01$ (the smallest tabulated $p$-value in `adf.test`).
- Because $p < 0.01$, we decisively reject the null hypothesis of a unit root at the $1\%$ significance level.
- **Methodological Decision:** The series is covariance-stationary in levels. Therefore, **no differencing is required** ($d = 0$). We proceed with stationary $\text{AR}(p)$ specifications in levels.

Consequently, the logarithmic lynx series is confirmed to be stationary in levels ($I(0)$). ## Step 4: Model Identification via ACF and PACF

### Purpose
To analyze empirical autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation functions to determine candidate autoregressive orders ($p$).

### Statistical Concept
For an $\text{AR}(p)$ process:
1. **Sample Autocorrelation Function (ACF):** Satisfies the Yule–Walker difference equations, generating damped exponential decay or damped sinusoidal waves.
2. **Sample Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF):** Measures the correlation between $Y_t$ and $Y_{t-k}$ after filtering out the linear dependence explained by intermediate lags $Y_{t-1}, \dots, Y_{t-k+1}$. Theoretical PACF cuts off sharply to zero for all lags $k > p$:

$$
\phi_{kk} = 0 \quad \text{for all } k > p.
$$

Under the null hypothesis of an $\text{AR}(p)$ process, sample PACF values beyond lag $p$ are asymptotically normal with variance $1/n$, yielding Bartlett 95% confidence intervals at:

$$
\text{CI}_{0.95} = \pm \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{n}}.
$$

## Step 5: Model Estimation and Parameter Significance

### Purpose
To estimate the parameters of the candidate models using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE), extract standard errors, test coefficient significance, and evaluate characteristic roots.

### Statistical Concept
Parameters are estimated via Maximum Likelihood Estimation initialized through the Kalman filter. The asymptotic covariance matrix of the estimators $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ is obtained from the inverse Fisher Information matrix:

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}) = \mathcal{I}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}).
$$

For each coefficient, we evaluate the Wald statistic:

$$
t = \frac{\hat{\phi}_j}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\phi}_j)} \stackrel{a}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
$$

Coefficients are statistically significant at $\alpha = 0.05$ if $|t| > 1.96$ ($p < 0.05$).

## Step 6: Multi-Criteria Model Selection

### Purpose
To compare candidate models across goodness-of-fit, parameter penalty criteria, and residual variance, explaining why model choice cannot rely on AIC alone.

### Statistical Concept
Goodness-of-fit naturally improves as additional lags are appended to an autoregressive model. Penalized likelihood criteria balance in-sample fit against model complexity:

$$
\text{AIC} = -2\ln(\hat{L}) + 2k,
$$

$$
\text{BIC} = -2\ln(\hat{L}) + k\ln(n),
$$

where $k$ is the total number of estimated parameters (including autoregressive parameters, constant, and innovation variance $\sigma_e^2$), and $n$ is the training sample size ($n = 100$).

### Why Model Selection Cannot Rely on AIC Alone

::: {.callout-note}
### Critical Distinction Between AIC and BIC
- **AIC is asymptotically efficient**; its penalty ($2k$) reflects the Kullback–Leibler information discrepancy. However, in small-to-moderate samples, AIC has a non-zero probability of **over-parameterizing** autoregressive processes because the penalty does not grow with sample size.
- **BIC is dimensionally consistent**; because $\ln(n) = \ln(100) \approx 4.605 > 2$, BIC penalizes additional coefficients more than twice as heavily as AIC. Under the true finite $\text{AR}(p)$ model, the probability that BIC selects the correct order approaches 1 as $n \to \infty$.
- Relying on AIC alone risks selecting overfitted specifications that capture in-sample noise at the expense of out-of-sample forecast accuracy.
:::

### R Implementation

## Step 7: Model Diagnostics and Residual Verification

### Purpose
To rigorously check that the residuals of the fitted $\text{AR}(2)$ model satisfy white noise assumptions: zero mean, homoscedasticity, absence of serial correlation, and approximate Gaussianity.

### Statistical Concept
Let $\hat{e}_t = Y_t - \hat{Y}_t$ be the model residuals.
1. **Ljung–Box Portmanteau Test:** Evaluates whether residual autocorrelations up to lag $K$ are jointly zero:
   $$
   Q^*(K) = n(n + 2) \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\hat{r}_k^2(\hat{e})}{n - k} \stackrel{H_0}{\sim} \chi^2(K - p),
   $$
   where degrees of freedom must be adjusted by subtracting the $p = 2$ estimated AR coefficients ($df = K - p$).
2. **Normality:** Assessed via the **Shapiro–Wilk test** and Normal Quantile–Quantile (Q–Q) plots.

## Step 8: Out-of-Sample Forecasting and Empirical Evaluation

### Purpose
To generate multi-step dynamic out-of-sample forecasts over the 14-year post-sample horizon (1921–1934), compute prediction intervals, and evaluate forecast accuracy against the competing specifications.

### Statistical Concept
For an $\text{AR}(2)$ process, point forecasts $\hat{Y}_{n+h|n}$ are computed recursively:

$$
\hat{Y}_{n+1|n} = c + \phi_1 Y_n + \phi_2 Y_{n-1},
$$

$$
\hat{Y}_{n+h|n} = c + \phi_1 \hat{Y}_{n+h-1|n} + \phi_2 \hat{Y}_{n+h-2|n} \quad (h \geq 2).
$$

The $100(1 - \alpha)\%$ prediction interval at horizon $h$ is given by:

$$
\hat{Y}_{n+h|n} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sigma_e \sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{h-1} \psi_j^2},
$$

where $\psi_j$ are the impulse-response coefficients from the Wold representation $Y_t = \mu + \sum_{j=0}^\infty \psi_j e_{t-j}$.

Out-of-sample performance is quantified on the reserved test partition ($h = 14$) using:

- **Mean Absolute Error (MAE):** $\frac{1}{h}\sum_{i=1}^h |Y_{n+i} - \hat{Y}_{n+i|n}|$
- **Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):** $\sqrt{\frac{1}{h}\sum_{i=1}^h (Y_{n+i} - \hat{Y}_{n+i|n})^2}$
- **Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE):** $\frac{100\%}{h}\sum_{i=1}^h \left|\frac{Y_{n+i} - \hat{Y}_{n+i|n}}{Y_{n+i}}\right|$

## Step 9: Academic Discussion, Limitations, and Defense Preparation

### Comprehensive Summary of Findings

1. **Identification:** The sample PACF exhibited a distinct two-lag cutoff ($\hat{\phi}_{11} = 0.780, \hat{\phi}_{22} = -0.711$), while the ACF showed damped sinusoidal oscillation with a 9.69-year cycle.
2. **Estimation & Stationarity:** The $\text{AR}(2)$ parameter estimates ($\hat{\phi}_1 = 1.3693, \hat{\phi}_2 = -0.7385$) possess complex roots with modulus $1.1637 > 1$, confirming covariance stationarity.
3. **Model Selection:** BIC ($16.16$) and parameter significance strictly favored $\text{AR}(2)$ over $\text{AR}(3)$ and $\text{AR}(4)$, avoiding the overfitting tendency of AIC.
4. **Validation:** Residuals satisfied all white noise criteria ($p > 0.10$ for Ljung–Box tests) and conformed to normality ($W = 0.991, p = 0.746$).
5. **Forecasting:** Out-of-sample validation confirmed $\text{AR}(2)$ as the most accurate predictive specification ($\text{RMSE} = 0.2824$).

### Methodological Limitations

- **Linearity vs Non-linear Dynamics:** The linear $\text{AR}(2)$ model assumes symmetric cycles. In predator–prey ecology, population declines are often steeper than the growth phase. Advanced literature (Tong, 1990) models this asymmetry using Self-Exciting Threshold Autoregressive (SETAR) models.
- **Asymptotic Normality:** Prediction intervals rely on Gaussian white noise innovations. While the logarithmic transformation normalized residuals, real-world extreme shocks can induce fat-tailed distributions requiring bootstrap prediction intervals.

### Anticipated Academic Defense Questions & Model Answers

::: {.callout-important}
### Question 1: Why did you not difference the series before fitting an AR model?
**Model Answer:** The Augmented Dickey–Fuller test on the training partition yielded $\text{DF} = -4.6713$ with $p < 0.01$, firmly rejecting the null hypothesis of a unit root. Differencing a series that is already stationary ($I(0)$) constitutes **overdifferencing**. Overdifferencing introduces an artificial moving average unit root ($\theta = 1$), increases residual variance, and degrades long-term forecasts.
:::

::: {.callout-important}
### Question 2: AR(4) achieved a lower AIC than AR(2). Why did you select AR(2)?
**Model Answer:** AIC penalizes complexity by $2k$, which has a well-known asymptotic probability of selecting over-parameterized models in finite samples. BIC uses $k\ln(n) \approx 4.605k$, which penalizes redundant parameters more severely and is consistent. In our data, the 3rd and 4th lag coefficients were statistically insignificant ($p > 0.05$). Furthermore, out-of-sample evaluation demonstrated that $\text{AR}(2)$ achieved lower MAE and RMSE than $\text{AR}(4)$, confirming that the lower AIC of $\text{AR}(4)$ was the result of in-sample overfitting.
:::

::: {.callout-important}
### Question 3: How do you justify the degrees of freedom used in the Ljung–Box test?
**Model Answer:** When testing the residuals of an estimated $\text{AR}(p)$ model, the test statistic does not follow a $\chi^2(K)$ distribution because the residuals are estimated from the data, which consumes degrees of freedom. Box and Pierce (1970) and Ljung and Box (1978) established that the null distribution is asymptotically $\chi^2(K - p)$. Failing to subtract the $p = 2$ estimated parameters would lead to overly conservative test statistics and inflated $p$-values.
:::
