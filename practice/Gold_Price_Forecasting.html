Executive Summary

Gold (XAU) occupies a singular role in quantitative finance, serving simultaneously as a global monetary reserve asset, a systemic risk hedge, an inflation counter-weight, and an actively traded industrial and jewelry commodity. Developing disciplined econometric forecasts of gold price dynamics is a critical priority for central banks, sovereign wealth institutions, bullion dealers, and financial engineers.

This study implements an academically rigorous and fully reproducible empirical investigation of gold price forecasting across five classical time-series specifications:

  1. Autoregressive Model — \(\text{AR}(p)\)
  2. Moving Average Model — \(\text{MA}(q)\)
  3. Autoregressive Moving Average Model — \(\text{ARMA}(p, q)\)
  4. Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average — \(\text{SARIMA}(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)[s]\)
  5. Simple Exponential Smoothing — \(\text{SES}\)

Using \(2,971\) daily market observations from Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME Group) COMEX Continuous Gold Futures (GC=F) spanning January 2015 to October 2026, we implement a strict chronological \(80\%/20\%\) in-sample estimation and out-of-sample holdout protocol.

Key empirical findings:

  • Non-Stationarity in Levels (\(I(1)\)): The Augmented Dickey–Fuller test on price levels yields \(\text{ADF} = -1.4591\) (\(p = 0.8073\)), failing to reject the presence of a unit root. First-differencing yields strong stationarity (\(\text{ADF} = -14.878, p < 0.01\)), confirming that the series requires integration order \(d=1\).
  • Holdout Predictive Superiority: On the unobserved 595-day out-of-sample test horizon (June 2024 to October 2026), \(\text{ARIMA}(0,1,0)\) with drift achieved the highest forecasting accuracy (\(\text{MAE} = \$1,164.18\), \(\text{RMSE} = \$1,390.75\), \(\text{MAPE} = 28.80\%\)). By estimating a positive drift term (\(\hat{c} \approx \$0.48\)/day), it captured the persistent secular rally in bullion prices driven by geopolitical stress and central bank de-dollarization.
  • Martingale Benchmark Validation: Classical zero-drift models (\(\text{SES}\), \(\text{AR}(1)\), \(\text{MA}(1)\), \(\text{ARMA}(1,1)\)) produced flat multi-step forecast trajectories (\(\text{RMSE} \approx \$1,548.94\), \(\text{MAPE} \approx 32.39\%\)), directly reflecting the Martingale Difference Hypothesis where the conditional expectation of future asset prices is dominated by the most recent price observation.

1 1. Introduction and Research Objectives

1.1 Background and Motivation

Gold holds a unique status in modern asset pricing: - As a physical commodity, its supply is constrained by mining extraction costs and scrap recycling, while physical demand is driven by jewelry and advanced electronics manufacturing. - As a monetary store of value, its price is fundamentally determined by macroeconomic drivers: real interest rates (U.S. TIPS yields), currency valuations (the U.S. Dollar Index, DXY), sovereign reserve accumulation, and global geopolitical tail risk.

Because market participants, treasury departments, and risk managers require objective, model-based price trajectory benchmarks, constructing and comparing time-series models within the classic Box–Jenkins framework is an indispensable analytical foundation.

1.2 Research Objectives

The specific empirical objectives are: 1. Conduct an exhaustive exploratory data analysis of historical gold prices, examining secular trends, empirical distributions (levels vs. log returns), annual volatility regimes, and descriptive statistics across the last five years. 2. Formally test for stationarity and determine the integration order \(d\) through graphical rolling statistics, autocorrelation analysis, and the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (\(\text{ADF}\)) unit root test. 3. Formulate, estimate, and select optimal candidate specifications for each of the five target model families using Information Criteria (\(\text{AIC}\), \(\text{BIC}\)) and residual diagnostics. 4. Execute uniform out-of-sample forecasts over an unobserved \(20\%\) chronological holdout sample under identical forecast horizons and origins. 5. Provide multi-metric evaluation (\(\text{MAE}\), \(\text{RMSE}\), \(\text{MAPE}\)), generate both publication-grade static figures and interactive ggplot2/plotly visualization suites, and synthesize financial and econometric conclusions.

2.2 Price Level vs. Return Differencing

In financial time series, analysts frequently distinguish between forecasting: 1. The Price Level (\(P_t\)): Non-stationary, persistent, and directly relevant for balance sheet valuation, budgeting, and long-term procurement. 2. Log Returns (\(r_t = 100 \times \ln(P_t / P_{t-1})\)) or First Differences (\(\Delta P_t = P_t - P_{t-1}\)): Stationary (\(I(0)\)), eliminating stochastic trend persistence.

As mandated by our objective, the primary forecasting target is the gold price level. When stationary models (\(\text{AR}\), \(\text{MA}\), \(\text{ARMA}\)) require differenced series for estimation, forecasts are integrated back to price levels to guarantee fair, out-of-sample comparability against integrated models (\(\text{ARIMA/SARIMA}\)) and \(\text{SES}\).

5 5. Stationarity Analysis

5.1 Theoretical Foundation

A stochastic time series \(\{Y_t\}\) is defined as weakly (covariance) stationary if and only if: 1. Constant Mean: \(E[Y_t] = \mu\) for all \(t\). 2. Constant Finite Variance: \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t) = \sigma^2 < \infty\) for all \(t\). 3. Time-Invariant Autocovariance: \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-k}) = \gamma_k\) depends exclusively on the displacement lag \(k\), and not on the absolute time index \(t\).

Financial asset price levels generally violate conditions (1) and (2) due to secular growth, inflation drift, and stochastic random walk trends (\(I(1)\) processes). Directly estimating autoregressive or moving average models on non-stationary levels produces spurious autocorrelations and invalid \(t\)-statistics.

5.3 Formal Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) Unit Root Test

The ADF test investigates the presence of a unit root using the regression: \[\Delta Y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma Y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \delta_i \Delta Y_{t-i} + e_t\]

  • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): \(\gamma = 0\) (the series contains a unit root, i.e., is non-stationary).
  • Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_1\)): \(\gamma < 0\) (the series is stationary).
  • Significance Threshold: \(\alpha = 0.05\).
Code 
adf_level <- adf.test(gold_df$price)
adf_diff  <- adf.test(diff(gold_df$price))

adf_comparison <- tibble(
  Series = c("Gold Price Level (P_t)", "First Difference (ΔP_t)"),
  `ADF Test Statistic` = c(sprintf("%.4f", adf_level$statistic), sprintf("%.4f", adf_diff$statistic)),
  `Lag Order`          = c(adf_level$parameter, adf_diff$parameter),
  `p-value`            = c(sprintf("%.4f", adf_level$p.value), "< 0.01"),
  `Decision (α = 0.05)` = c("Fail to Reject H0 (Non-Stationary)", "Reject H0 (Stationary)"),
  `Integration Order`  = c("I(1)", "I(0)")
)

6 6. ACF/PACF and Model Identification

6.1 Diagnostic Autocorrelation Functions

Theoretical signature profiles in the Box–Jenkins methodology: - \(\text{AR}(p)\): Partial Autocorrelation Function (\(\text{PACF}\)) cuts off abruptly after lag \(p\), while the Autocorrelation Function (\(\text{ACF}\)) decays exponentially or in a damped oscillatory pattern. - \(\text{MA}(q)\): \(\text{ACF}\) cuts off abruptly after lag \(q\), while the \(\text{PACF}\) decays exponentially. - \(\text{ARMA}(p, q)\): Both \(\text{ACF}\) and \(\text{PACF}\) display gradual tailing-off behavior.

7 7. Chronological Training and Test Partitioning

8.5 Model 5: Simple Exponential Smoothing (SES)

Simple Exponential Smoothing models a series with a time-varying level but without an explicit linear trend or seasonal component:

10.2 Accuracy Metrics Formulation

We evaluate predictive performance on actual gold price levels using three standard metrics:

  1. Mean Absolute Error (MAE): \[ \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n_{\text{test}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\text{test}}} |Y_t - \hat{Y}_t| \]

  2. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): \[ \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{\text{test}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\text{test}}} (Y_t - \hat{Y}_t)^2} \]

  3. Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE): \[ \text{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{n_{\text{test}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\text{test}}} \left|\frac{Y_t - \hat{Y}_t}{Y_t}\right| \]

Code 
y_test <- test_data$price

calc_accuracy <- function(actual, predicted) {
  err <- actual - predicted
  mae  <- mean(abs(err))
  rmse <- sqrt(mean(err^2))
  mape <- mean(abs(err / actual)) * 100
  c(MAE = mae, RMSE = rmse, MAPE = mape)
}

metrics_matrix <- rbind(

13 13. Discussion and Financial Interpretation

13.1 1. Superiority of the Drift Specification

The automated SARIMA selection chose \(\text{ARIMA}(0, 1, 0)\) with a positive drift term (\(\hat{c} \approx \$0.48\)/day). This specification outperformed all zero-drift competitors by \(\$141.11\)/oz in MAE and \(\$158.19\)/oz in RMSE. Because physical gold experienced a sustained secular bull market driven by global central bank reserve purchases and structural inflation, incorporating the historical drift allowed the model to project an upward path rather than a static horizontal line.

13.2 2. Martingale Behavior in Zero-Drift Models

The zero-drift models (\(\text{SES}\), \(\text{AR}(1)\), \(\text{MA}(1)\), \(\text{ARMA}(1,1)\)) produced nearly identical flat forecast trajectories (\(\text{RMSE} \approx \$1,548.94\)). This behavior is consistent with the Martingale Difference Hypothesis (MDH) in financial asset pricing: \[ E[P_{t+h} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = P_t \] In the absence of a drift parameter, any transient \(\text{AR}\) or \(\text{MA}\) shock decays rapidly within a few steps, causing long-horizon point forecasts to collapse to the last observed price level.

13.3 3. Role of Simple Exponential Smoothing as a Benchmark

\(\text{SES}\) estimated an optimal smoothing parameter of \(\alpha \approx 0.999\), indicating that the model places virtually all weight on the most recent observation. While mathematically parsimonious, SES serves as an essential baseline: any complex multi-parameter econometric model that fails to outperform SES imposes unwarranted parameter uncertainty without predictive reward.

14 14. Limitations

  1. Univariate Information Constraint: Univariate time-series models rely exclusively on past price history, ignoring exogenous macroeconomic drivers:
    • Real interest rates (U.S. 10-year TIPS yield).
    • Foreign exchange dynamics (U.S. Dollar Index, DXY).
    • Global geopolitical risk indices and central bank net purchases.
  2. Conditional Homoskedasticity: Classical Box–Jenkins models assume constant disturbance variance \(\sigma^2\). As shown in the return distribution analysis, gold displays substantial volatility clustering, motivating hybrid \(\text{ARIMA-GARCH}\) specifications.
  3. Multi-Step Uncertainty: Point forecasts over extended horizons (595 days) exhibit rapidly widening prediction intervals, reflecting the fundamental reality of market efficiency over multi-year horizons.

15 15. Conclusion

This study executed a comprehensive, reproducible empirical comparison of five classical time-series models for gold price forecasting using 2,971 daily CME COMEX gold futures records (2015–2026).

  • Unit Root Evidence: Gold price levels are non-stationary (\(I(1)\)), necessitating first-order differencing.
  • Top Out-of-Sample Model: \(\text{ARIMA}(0,1,0)\) with drift won the evaluation (\(\text{RMSE} = \$1,390.75\), \(\text{MAE} = \$1,164.18\), \(\text{MAPE} = 28.80\%\)).
  • Practical Takeaway: While statistical time-series models provide disciplined baselines for financial engineers, real-world hedging and portfolio management require combining univariate drift models with macroeconomic risk drivers and dynamic volatility modeling.

## Executive Summary {.unnumbered}

Gold (*XAU*) occupies a singular role in quantitative finance, serving simultaneously as a global monetary reserve asset, a systemic risk hedge, an inflation counter-weight, and an actively traded industrial and jewelry commodity. Developing disciplined econometric forecasts of gold price dynamics is a critical priority for central banks, sovereign wealth institutions, bullion dealers, and financial engineers.

This study implements an academically rigorous and fully reproducible empirical investigation of **gold price forecasting** across five classical time-series specifications:

1. **Autoregressive Model — $\text{AR}(p)$**
2. **Moving Average Model — $\text{MA}(q)$**
3. **Autoregressive Moving Average Model — $\text{ARMA}(p, q)$**
4. **Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average — $\text{SARIMA}(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)[s]$**
5. **Simple Exponential Smoothing — $\text{SES}$**

Using **$2,971$ daily market observations** from Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME Group) COMEX Continuous Gold Futures (`GC=F`) spanning **January 2015 to October 2026**, we implement a strict chronological $80\%/20\%$ in-sample estimation and out-of-sample holdout protocol.

Key empirical findings:

* **Non-Stationarity in Levels ($I(1)$):** The Augmented Dickey–Fuller test on price levels yields $\text{ADF} = -1.4591$ ($p = 0.8073$), failing to reject the presence of a unit root. First-differencing yields strong stationarity ($\text{ADF} = -14.878, p < 0.01$), confirming that the series requires integration order $d=1$.
* **Holdout Predictive Superiority:** On the unobserved 595-day out-of-sample test horizon (June 2024 to October 2026), **$\text{ARIMA}(0,1,0)$ with drift** achieved the highest forecasting accuracy ($\text{MAE} = \$1,164.18$, $\text{RMSE} = \$1,390.75$, $\text{MAPE} = 28.80\%$). By estimating a positive drift term ($\hat{c} \approx \$0.48$/day), it captured the persistent secular rally in bullion prices driven by geopolitical stress and central bank de-dollarization.
* **Martingale Benchmark Validation:** Classical zero-drift models ($\text{SES}$, $\text{AR}(1)$, $\text{MA}(1)$, $\text{ARMA}(1,1)$) produced flat multi-step forecast trajectories ($\text{RMSE} \approx \$1,548.94$, $\text{MAPE} \approx 32.39\%$), directly reflecting the Martingale Difference Hypothesis where the conditional expectation of future asset prices is dominated by the most recent price observation.

## 1. Introduction and Research Objectives

### Background and Motivation
Gold holds a unique status in modern asset pricing:
- As a **physical commodity**, its supply is constrained by mining extraction costs and scrap recycling, while physical demand is driven by jewelry and advanced electronics manufacturing.
- As a **monetary store of value**, its price is fundamentally determined by macroeconomic drivers: real interest rates (U.S. TIPS yields), currency valuations (the U.S. Dollar Index, DXY), sovereign reserve accumulation, and global geopolitical tail risk.

Because market participants, treasury departments, and risk managers require objective, model-based price trajectory benchmarks, constructing and comparing time-series models within the classic Box–Jenkins framework is an indispensable analytical foundation.

The specific empirical objectives are:
1. Conduct an exhaustive exploratory data analysis of historical gold prices, examining secular trends, empirical distributions (levels vs. log returns), annual volatility regimes, and descriptive statistics across the last five years.

4. Execute uniform out-of-sample forecasts over an unobserved $20\%$ chronological holdout sample under identical forecast horizons and origins.
5. Provide multi-metric evaluation ($\text{MAE}$, $\text{RMSE}$, $\text{MAPE}$), generate both publication-grade static figures and interactive `ggplot2`/`plotly` visualization suites, and synthesize financial and econometric conclusions.

### Price Level vs. Return Differencing
In financial time series, analysts frequently distinguish between forecasting:
1. **The Price Level ($P_t$):** Non-stationary, persistent, and directly relevant for balance sheet valuation, budgeting, and long-term procurement.
2. **Log Returns ($r_t = 100 \times \ln(P_t / P_{t-1})$) or First Differences ($\Delta P_t = P_t - P_{t-1}$):** Stationary ($I(0)$), eliminating stochastic trend persistence.

As mandated by our objective, the primary forecasting target is the gold price level. When stationary models ($\text{AR}$, $\text{MA}$, $\text{ARMA}$) require differenced series for estimation, forecasts are integrated back to price levels to guarantee fair, out-of-sample comparability against integrated models ($\text{ARIMA/SARIMA}$) and $\text{SES}$.

## 5. Stationarity Analysis

### Theoretical Foundation
A stochastic time series $\{Y_t\}$ is defined as **weakly (covariance) stationary** if and only if:
1. **Constant Mean:** $E[Y_t] = \mu$ for all $t$.
2. **Constant Finite Variance:** $\operatorname{Var}(Y_t) = \sigma^2 < \infty$ for all $t$.
3. **Time-Invariant Autocovariance:** $\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-k}) = \gamma_k$ depends exclusively on the displacement lag $k$, and not on the absolute time index $t$.

Financial asset price levels generally violate conditions (1) and (2) due to secular growth, inflation drift, and stochastic random walk trends ($I(1)$ processes). Directly estimating autoregressive or moving average models on non-stationary levels produces spurious autocorrelations and invalid $t$-statistics.

### Formal Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) Unit Root Test
The ADF test investigates the presence of a unit root using the regression:
$$\Delta Y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma Y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \delta_i \Delta Y_{t-i} + e_t$$

- **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** $\gamma = 0$ (the series contains a unit root, i.e., is non-stationary).
- **Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$):** $\gamma < 0$ (the series is stationary).
- **Significance Threshold:** $\alpha = 0.05$.

```{r}
#| label: adf-test-results
adf_level <- adf.test(gold_df$price)
adf_diff  <- adf.test(diff(gold_df$price))

adf_comparison <- tibble(
  Series = c("Gold Price Level (P_t)", "First Difference (ΔP_t)"),
  `ADF Test Statistic` = c(sprintf("%.4f", adf_level$statistic), sprintf("%.4f", adf_diff$statistic)),
  `Lag Order`          = c(adf_level$parameter, adf_diff$parameter),
  `p-value`            = c(sprintf("%.4f", adf_level$p.value), "< 0.01"),
  `Decision (α = 0.05)` = c("Fail to Reject H0 (Non-Stationary)", "Reject H0 (Stationary)"),
  `Integration Order`  = c("I(1)", "I(0)")
)

kable(adf_comparison, caption = "Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) Unit Root Test Summary")
```

The statistical decision is unequivocal: the price level exhibits unit root behavior ($p = 0.8073$), whereas first-differencing renders the series strictly covariance stationary ($p < 0.01$). Hence, models must incorporate integration order $d = 1$.

## 6. ACF/PACF and Model Identification

### Diagnostic Autocorrelation Functions
Theoretical signature profiles in the Box–Jenkins methodology:
- **$\text{AR}(p)$:** Partial Autocorrelation Function ($\text{PACF}$) cuts off abruptly after lag $p$, while the Autocorrelation Function ($\text{ACF}$) decays exponentially or in a damped oscillatory pattern.
- **$\text{MA}(q)$:** $\text{ACF}$ cuts off abruptly after lag $q$, while the $\text{PACF}$ decays exponentially.
- **$\text{ARMA}(p, q)$:** Both $\text{ACF}$ and $\text{PACF}$ display gradual tailing-off behavior.

## 8. Estimation of the Five Time-Series Models

All model specifications are estimated strictly on the in-sample training window `train_data$price`.

### Model 1: Autoregressive Model — AR(p)
An $\text{AR}(p)$ model fitted to the integrated series corresponds to $\text{ARIMA}(p, 1, 0)$:

$$ (1 - \phi_1 B - \dots - \phi_p B^p)(1 - B)Y_t = c + \varepsilon_t $$

```{r}
#| label: model-ar
fit_ar1 <- Arima(train_data$price, order = c(1, 1, 0))
summary(fit_ar1)
```

### Model 2: Moving Average Model — MA(q)
An $\text{MA}(q)$ model fitted to the integrated series corresponds to $\text{ARIMA}(0, 1, q)$:

$$ (1 - B)Y_t = \mu + (1 + \theta_1 B + \dots + \theta_q B^q)\varepsilon_t $$

```{r}
#| label: model-ma
fit_ma1 <- Arima(train_data$price, order = c(0, 1, 1))
summary(fit_ma1)
```

### Model 3: Autoregressive Moving Average — ARMA(p, q)
Combining autoregressive persistence with shock accommodation yields $\text{ARIMA}(p, 1, q)$:

$$ (1 - \phi_1 B)(1 - B)Y_t = c + (1 + \theta_1 B)\varepsilon_t $$

```{r}
#| label: model-arma
fit_arma11 <- Arima(train_data$price, order = c(1, 1, 1))
summary(fit_arma11)
```

### Model 4: Seasonal ARIMA — SARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)[s]
We utilize `auto.arima()` with stepwise search across the parameter space, allowing drift and seasonal candidates. For daily data, the algorithm selects the most parsimonious specification minimizing the corrected Akaike Information Criterion ($\text{AICc}$).

```{r}
#| label: model-sarima
fit_sarima <- auto.arima(
  train_data$price,
  seasonal = TRUE,
  stepwise = TRUE,
  approximation = FALSE
)
summary(fit_sarima)
```

> [!NOTE]
> `auto.arima()` selects **$\text{ARIMA}(0, 1, 0)$ with drift**, estimating an average historical drift coefficient of $\hat{c} \approx \$0.4766$/day over the training window.

### Model 5: Simple Exponential Smoothing (SES)
Simple Exponential Smoothing models a series with a time-varying level but without an explicit linear trend or seasonal component:

$$ \ell_t = \alpha Y_t + (1 - \alpha)\ell_{t-1}, \qquad 0 < \alpha < 1 $$

Point forecasts from SES are constant flat projections over the entire forecast horizon: $\hat{Y}_{T+h|T} = \ell_T$.

```{r}
#| label: model-ses
fit_ses <- ses(train_data$price, h = n_test)
summary(fit_ses$model)
```

## 9. Residual Diagnostics

To verify model adequacy, residuals must not exhibit remaining linear serial correlation. We conduct the **Ljung–Box portmanteau test** up to lag $K = 20$, subtracting estimated model parameters $(p + q)$ from degrees of freedom.

```{r}
#| label: residual-diagnostics-table
models_list <- list(
  "AR(1) integrated"    = list(fit = fit_ar1,    p = 1, q = 0),
  "MA(1) integrated"    = list(fit = fit_ma1,    p = 0, q = 1),
  "ARMA(1,1) integrated"= list(fit = fit_arma11, p = 1, q = 1),
  "SARIMA (Auto ARIMA)" = list(fit = fit_sarima, p = 0, q = 0),
  "SES"                 = list(fit = fit_ses$model, p = 1, q = 0)
)

lb_results <- tibble(
  Model = names(models_list),
  `Ljung-Box Q*` = map_dbl(models_list, ~{
    res <- na.omit(residuals(.x$fit))
    Box.test(res, lag = 20, type = "Ljung-Box")$statistic
  }),
  `df` = map_int(models_list, ~{
    as.integer(20 - (.x$p + .x$q))
  }),
  `p-value` = map_dbl(models_list, ~{
    res <- na.omit(residuals(.x$fit))
    dof <- max(1, 20 - (.x$p + .x$q))
    Box.test(res, lag = 20, type = "Ljung-Box", fitdf = .x$p + .x$q)$p.value
  }),
  `Diagnostic Conclusion` = ifelse(`p-value` > 0.05, "Adequate (No serial correlation)", "Serial correlation detected")
)
```

### Accuracy Metrics Formulation
We evaluate predictive performance on actual gold price levels using three standard metrics:

1. **Mean Absolute Error (MAE):**
   $$ \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n_{\text{test}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\text{test}}} |Y_t - \hat{Y}_t| $$

2. **Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):**
   $$ \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{\text{test}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\text{test}}} (Y_t - \hat{Y}_t)^2} $$

3. **Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE):**
   $$ \text{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{n_{\text{test}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\text{test}}} \left|\frac{Y_t - \hat{Y}_t}{Y_t}\right| $$

```{r}
#| label: compute-evaluation-metrics
y_test <- test_data$price

calc_accuracy <- function(actual, predicted) {
  err <- actual - predicted
  mae  <- mean(abs(err))
  rmse <- sqrt(mean(err^2))
  mape <- mean(abs(err / actual)) * 100
  c(MAE = mae, RMSE = rmse, MAPE = mape)
}

metrics_matrix <- rbind(
  `AR(1) integrated`     = calc_accuracy(y_test, as.numeric(fc_ar$mean)),

  `SARIMA (Auto ARIMA)`  = calc_accuracy(y_test, as.numeric(fc_sarima$mean)),
  `Simple Exponential Smoothing (SES)` = calc_accuracy(y_test, as.numeric(fc_ses$mean))
```

## 13. Discussion and Financial Interpretation

### 1. Superiority of the Drift Specification
The automated SARIMA selection chose $\text{ARIMA}(0, 1, 0)$ with a positive drift term ($\hat{c} \approx \$0.48$/day). This specification outperformed all zero-drift competitors by $\$141.11$/oz in MAE and $\$158.19$/oz in RMSE. Because physical gold experienced a sustained secular bull market driven by global central bank reserve purchases and structural inflation, incorporating the historical drift allowed the model to project an upward path rather than a static horizontal line.

### 2. Martingale Behavior in Zero-Drift Models
The zero-drift models ($\text{SES}$, $\text{AR}(1)$, $\text{MA}(1)$, $\text{ARMA}(1,1)$) produced nearly identical flat forecast trajectories ($\text{RMSE} \approx \$1,548.94$). This behavior is consistent with the **Martingale Difference Hypothesis (MDH)** in financial asset pricing:
$$ E[P_{t+h} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = P_t $$
In the absence of a drift parameter, any transient $\text{AR}$ or $\text{MA}$ shock decays rapidly within a few steps, causing long-horizon point forecasts to collapse to the last observed price level.

### 3. Role of Simple Exponential Smoothing as a Benchmark
$\text{SES}$ estimated an optimal smoothing parameter of $\alpha \approx 0.999$, indicating that the model places virtually all weight on the most recent observation. While mathematically parsimonious, SES serves as an essential baseline: any complex multi-parameter econometric model that fails to outperform SES imposes unwarranted parameter uncertainty without predictive reward.

## 14. Limitations

1. **Univariate Information Constraint:** Univariate time-series models rely exclusively on past price history, ignoring exogenous macroeconomic drivers:
   - Real interest rates (U.S. 10-year TIPS yield).
   - Foreign exchange dynamics (U.S. Dollar Index, DXY).
   - Global geopolitical risk indices and central bank net purchases.
2. **Conditional Homoskedasticity:** Classical Box–Jenkins models assume constant disturbance variance $\sigma^2$. As shown in the return distribution analysis, gold displays substantial **volatility clustering**, motivating hybrid $\text{ARIMA-GARCH}$ specifications.
3. **Multi-Step Uncertainty:** Point forecasts over extended horizons (595 days) exhibit rapidly widening prediction intervals, reflecting the fundamental reality of market efficiency over multi-year horizons.

## 15. Conclusion

This study executed a comprehensive, reproducible empirical comparison of five classical time-series models for gold price forecasting using 2,971 daily CME COMEX gold futures records (2015–2026).

- **Unit Root Evidence:** Gold price levels are non-stationary ($I(1)$), necessitating first-order differencing.
- **Top Out-of-Sample Model:** **$\text{ARIMA}(0,1,0)$ with drift** won the evaluation ($\text{RMSE} = \$1,390.75$, $\text{MAE} = \$1,164.18$, $\text{MAPE} = 28.80\%$).
- **Practical Takeaway:** While statistical time-series models provide disciplined baselines for financial engineers, real-world hedging and portfolio management require combining univariate drift models with macroeconomic risk drivers and dynamic volatility modeling.
