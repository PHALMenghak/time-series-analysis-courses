1 Scope and analytical approach

This report answers Exercises 1–6 of Task 2 in I5TSA — TD/TP 1: Introduction, issued by Dr. Sim Tepmony and Mr. Nhim Malai. Tasks 1 (quiz) and 3 (take-home assignment) are outside the scope of this revision. The TSA package identifies its companion textbook as Cryer and Chan, Time Series Analysis with Applications in R, second edition.

The required plots are followed by evidence-based interpretations. Additional summaries, normal quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots, and autocorrelation functions (ACFs) are exploratory supplements, not additional handout questions. The textbook exhibits themselves were not supplied: the plots below reproduce the requested series and plotting conventions without claiming an exact graphical facsimile.

Three distinctions guide the analysis:

  • Time dependence concerns how observations relate across time. Joining points preserves their order but does not imply that intervening values were observed.
  • Marginal distribution describes values at an individual time: for example, Gaussian, chi-square, or Student’s \(t\). Nonnormality does not imply dependence.
  • Sample appearance varies between realizations. Short runs, clusters, and extreme observations can occur in an independent process.

observed_summary <- function(x) {
  c(N = length(x), Mean = mean(x), Median = median(x), SD = sd(x),
    Minimum = min(x), Maximum = max(x), `Lag-1 ACF` = lag1(x))
}
knitr::kable(t(observed_summary(larain)), digits = 3, row.names = FALSE)

Interpretation. Rainfall fluctuates substantially: the observed minimum is 4.08 inches in 1953, and the maximum is 40.29 inches in 1884. The mean (14.89 inches) exceeds the median (14.14 inches), consistent with the influence of unusually wet years. These are extremes relative to this sample; the plot does not establish measurement errors or justify deleting them.

There is no clear sustained monotonic trend in this plot. Because observations are annual totals, within-year seasonality cannot be assessed. The lag-1 sample ACF is -0.033, indicating little observed linear association between adjacent years. A single coefficient, or an absence of obvious visual pattern, does not establish independence, stationarity, or the absence of longer-term climatic variation.

4.1 Supplement · ACFs of the observed series

For lag \(k\), the sample ACF used here is

\[ \widehat\rho(k)= \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}(x_t-\bar x)(x_{t-k}-\bar x)} {\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t-\bar x)^2}. \]

It describes linear association, not every form of dependence. The dashed limits in Figure 3 are approximate pointwise white-noise reference limits, about \(\pm1.96/\sqrt{n}\). They are not simultaneous limits across all lags and do not constitute a test of independence. Interpretation also presumes a reasonably stable mean and variance; a changing mean can itself produce a large ACF. See the R ACF documentation.

5 Framework for Exercises 3–5

A completely random process here means independent and identically distributed (iid) observations:

\[ X_t \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} F,\qquad t=1,\ldots,48. \]

With finite variance, \(E(X_t)=\mu\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\sigma^2\), and \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-k})=0\) for nonzero \(k\). An iid process is strictly stationary and, with finite variance, weakly stationary. These are properties of the generating model, not conclusions established by inspecting 48 values.

Under the zero-mean convention, weak white noise has zero mean, constant finite variance, and zero autocovariance at nonzero lags. Independence is stronger than being uncorrelated. The normal and \(t_5\) simulations have zero mean; the raw chi-square simulation has mean 2, and its centered version \(X_t-2\) is white noise under this convention.

Model Mean Variance Marginal shape
\(N(0,1)\) \(0\) \(1\) Symmetric, Gaussian
\(\chi^2_2\) \(2\) \(4\) Nonnegative, right-skewed
\(t_5\) \(0\) \(5/3\) Symmetric, heavier tails than Gaussian

We generate three realizations of length 48 per distribution. Each seed is set once before replicate(), allowing the generator state to advance between realizations. Resetting the same seed inside each repetition would reproduce the same path. All generated paths are retained without selecting those that look most persuasive.

The helper displays each realization separately, using common vertical limits within each distribution. A paired normal Q–Q plot examines marginal shape: approximately straight points are compatible with a normal location-scale family, while systematic curvature suggests departures. It cannot diagnose temporal independence. Its line passes through sample quartiles and is not a confidence band.

Code 
n <- 48L
B <- 3L

plot_runs <- function(runs, distribution, theoretical_mean) {
  old <- par(mfrow = c(ncol(runs), 2), mar = c(3.6, 4, 2.5, 1),
             mgp = c(2.2, 0.7, 0))
  on.exit(par(old))
  limits <- range(runs, theoretical_mean)
  for (j in seq_len(ncol(runs))) {
    x <- runs[, j]
    plot(seq_along(x), x, type = "o", pch = 16, cex = 0.5,
         col = ink, ylim = limits, xlab = "Time index", ylab = "Value",
         main = paste(distribution, "- realization", j))
    abline(h = theoretical_mean, col = accent, lty = 2)
    stats::qqnorm(x, pch = 16, cex = 0.55, col = ink,
                  ylim = limits, main = paste("Normal Q-Q - realization", j),
                  xlab = "Theoretical normal quantile", ylab = "Sample quantile")
    stats::qqline(x, col = accent, lty = 2)
  }
}

run_summary <- function(runs) {
  data.frame(
    Realization = seq_len(ncol(runs)), N = nrow(runs),
    Mean = colMeans(runs), Variance = apply(runs, 2, var),
    Minimum = apply(runs, 2, min), Maximum = apply(runs, 2, max),
    `Lag-1 ACF` = apply(runs, 2, lag1), check.names = FALSE
  )
}

6 Exercise 3 · Independent normal observations

Question. Generate 48 independent normal values, plot them, and repeat. Does the series look random?

We choose \(X_t\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1)\), making the unspecified normal parameters explicit. Thus \(E(X_t)=0\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=1\), and \(\rho(k)=0\) for \(k\ne0\).

Code 
set.seed(2025)
normal_runs <- replicate(B, stats::rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = 1))
plot_runs(normal_runs, "N(0, 1)", theoretical_mean = 0)
Code 
knitr::kable(run_summary(normal_runs), digits = 3)

Answer. The realizations fluctuate irregularly around zero, and apparent runs or local rises and falls differ between realizations. This appearance is compatible with randomness, but independence is specified by the simulation model, not established by the visual impression. A random process need not alternate signs regularly or have sample mean exactly zero.

Across these realizations, sample means range from -0.338 to 0.128, and sample variances range from 0.892 to 1.034. Such differences reflect sampling variation. The standard deviation of a sample mean is \(1/\sqrt{48}\approx0.144\), so moderate deviations from zero are expected. Q–Q departures at the extremes of a small sample do not, by themselves, establish that the specified normal generator is incorrect.

7 Exercise 4 · Independent chi-square observations

Question. Repeat with 48 independent chi-square values with two degrees of freedom. Does it look random and nonnormal?

For \(X_t\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\chi^2_2\),

\[ E(X_t)=2,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_t)=4,\qquad f(x)=\tfrac12 e^{-x/2}\quad(x>0). \]

This is an exponential distribution with mean 2 and skewness 2. The R chi-square documentation gives the generator and distribution formulas.

Code 
set.seed(2026)
chisq_runs <- replicate(B, stats::rchisq(n, df = 2))
plot_runs(chisq_runs, "Chi-square(2)", theoretical_mean = 2)
Code 
knitr::kable(run_summary(chisq_runs), digits = 3)

Answer. The timing of high and low observations is irregular, as expected for iid draws. Nonnegative values and occasional large positive spikes reveal asymmetry more readily than in the normal experiment. The maximum over the three retained paths is 11.09; this is a simulation result, not an upper bound on the distribution. Curvature in the normal Q–Q plots helps distinguish this skewed marginal distribution from a Gaussian one.

These paths can look random and nonnormal because the properties concern different aspects of the process. Spikes do not imply serial dependence or data errors. The raw process has a positive mean; \(\varepsilon_t=X_t-2\) is zero-mean iid noise with variance 4. Subtracting 2 changes its location but neither its independence nor its skewness. The standard deviation of the sample mean is \(2/\sqrt{48}\approx0.289\), twice that of the standard-normal experiment.

8 Exercise 5 · Independent Student’s t observations

Question. Repeat with 48 independent \(t\) values with five degrees of freedom. Does it look random and nonnormal?

For \(X_t\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}t_5\),

\[ E(X_t)=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\frac{5}{5-2}=\frac53. \]

The distribution is symmetric with heavier tails than a normal distribution. Its excess kurtosis is \(6/(5-4)=6\), compared with zero for a Gaussian distribution. See the R Student’s t documentation for the generator, density, and mean/variance conditions.

Code 
set.seed(2027)
t_runs <- replicate(B, stats::rt(n, df = 5))
plot_runs(t_runs, "Student t(5)", theoretical_mean = 0)
Code 
knitr::kable(run_summary(t_runs), digits = 3)

Answer. As in the normal experiment, paths fluctuate irregularly around zero. The largest observed absolute value across these paths is 9.05. The heavy-tailed model makes large excursions more likely but does not guarantee an obvious extreme in every 48 observations. A short \(t_5\) path may therefore resemble a normal path; nonnormality is less visually apparent than for \(\chi^2_2\).

When sufficiently extreme observations occur, a normal Q–Q plot tends to depart outward in the tails. At this sample size the pattern can be weak or uneven. We know the simulated population is nonnormal from its generating model, not from whether a particular realization passes a visual or formal normality check.

A fair comparison of tails. The raw \(t_5\) distribution also has greater variance than \(N(0,1)\), so larger raw excursions alone do not isolate tail shape. Set \(Z_t=\sqrt{3/5}\,X_t\) to give the \(t\) variable unit variance. Compare the probability of exceeding three population standard deviations in absolute value:

Code 
tail_probability <- c(
  2 * stats::pnorm(3, lower.tail = FALSE),
  2 * stats::pt(3 * sqrt(5 / 3), df = 5, lower.tail = FALSE)
)
knitr::kable(data.frame(
  Distribution = c("Standard normal", "t(5) scaled by sqrt(3/5)"),
  `P(|Z| > 3)` = tail_probability, check.names = FALSE
), digits = 5)

This calculation separates heavier tails from the variance difference. The required time plots remain on their original scales; vertical limits are shared within a distribution, not across distributions.

8.1 Supplement · Serial correlation in simulated paths

Code 
local({
  old <- par(mfrow = c(1, 3), mar = c(4, 4, 3, 1))
  on.exit(par(old))
  examples <- list("N(0, 1)" = normal_runs[, 1],
                   "Chi-square(2)" = chisq_runs[, 1],
                   "Student t(5)" = t_runs[, 1])
  for (nm in names(examples)) {
    stats::acf(examples[[nm]], lag.max = 10, ylim = c(-1, 1),
               main = nm, xlab = "Lag (observations)", col = ink)
  }
})

The population ACF is zero at every positive lag for all three models, but the sample ACF need not be zero. R centers observations when estimating this ACF, including the raw chi-square values. With only 48 observations, the reference limits are approximately $$0.283; isolated crossings can occur by chance, especially when many lags or realizations are inspected. These limits are asymptotic and warrant caution for short, skewed, or heavy-tailed samples. No search for a favorable seed or repeated significance testing is used to certify independence.

10 Conclusions

The observed series illustrate annual rainfall variation, persistence in industrial color measurements, and strong annual seasonality in monthly temperatures. The simulations show that independent observations can have Gaussian, skewed, or heavy-tailed distributions. Known population properties must be distinguished from chance features in short realizations. Time plots address temporal structure; Q–Q plots address marginal shape; neither alone proves independence or stationarity.

## Scope and analytical approach

This report answers **Exercises 1–6 of Task 2** in *I5TSA — TD/TP 1: Introduction*, issued by Dr. Sim Tepmony and Mr. Nhim Malai. Tasks 1 (quiz) and 3 (take-home assignment) are outside the scope of this revision. The `TSA` package identifies its companion textbook as Cryer and Chan, *Time Series Analysis with Applications in R*, second edition.

The required plots are followed by evidence-based interpretations. Additional summaries, normal quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots, and autocorrelation functions (ACFs) are **exploratory supplements**, not additional handout questions. The textbook exhibits themselves were not supplied: the plots below reproduce the requested series and plotting conventions without claiming an exact graphical facsimile.

Three distinctions guide the analysis:

- **Time dependence** concerns how observations relate across time. Joining points preserves their order but does not imply that intervening values were observed.
- **Marginal distribution** describes values at an individual time: for example, Gaussian, chi-square, or Student's $t$. Nonnormality does not imply dependence.
- **Sample appearance** varies between realizations. Short runs, clusters, and extreme observations can occur in an independent process.

### Supplement · ACFs of the observed series

For lag $k$, the sample ACF used here is

$$
\widehat\rho(k)=
\frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}(x_t-\bar x)(x_{t-k}-\bar x)}
     {\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t-\bar x)^2}.
$$

It describes linear association, not every form of dependence. The dashed limits in @fig-observed-acf are approximate **pointwise** white-noise reference limits, about $\pm1.96/\sqrt{n}$. They are not simultaneous limits across all lags and do not constitute a test of independence. Interpretation also presumes a reasonably stable mean and variance; a changing mean can itself produce a large ACF. See the [R ACF documentation](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/acf.html).

## Framework for Exercises 3–5

A *completely random process* here means independent and identically distributed (iid) observations:

$$
X_t \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} F,\qquad t=1,\ldots,48.
$$

With finite variance, $E(X_t)=\mu$, $\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\sigma^2$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t-k})=0$ for nonzero $k$. An iid process is strictly stationary and, with finite variance, weakly stationary. These are properties of the generating model, not conclusions established by inspecting 48 values.

Under the zero-mean convention, **weak white noise** has zero mean, constant finite variance, and zero autocovariance at nonzero lags. Independence is stronger than being uncorrelated. The normal and $t_5$ simulations have zero mean; the raw chi-square simulation has mean 2, and its centered version $X_t-2$ is white noise under this convention.

| Model | Mean | Variance | Marginal shape |
|:--|--:|--:|:--|
| $N(0,1)$ | $0$ | $1$ | Symmetric, Gaussian |
| $\chi^2_2$ | $2$ | $4$ | Nonnegative, right-skewed |
| $t_5$ | $0$ | $5/3$ | Symmetric, heavier tails than Gaussian |

We generate **three realizations of length 48 per distribution**. Each seed is set once before `replicate()`, allowing the generator state to advance between realizations. Resetting the same seed inside each repetition would reproduce the same path. All generated paths are retained without selecting those that look most persuasive.

The helper displays each realization separately, using common vertical limits within each distribution. A paired normal Q–Q plot examines marginal shape: approximately straight points are compatible with a normal location-scale family, while systematic curvature suggests departures. It cannot diagnose temporal independence. Its line passes through sample quartiles and is not a confidence band.

```{r}
#| label: simulation-helpers
n <- 48L
B <- 3L

plot_runs <- function(runs, distribution, theoretical_mean) {
  old <- par(mfrow = c(ncol(runs), 2), mar = c(3.6, 4, 2.5, 1),
             mgp = c(2.2, 0.7, 0))
  on.exit(par(old))
  limits <- range(runs, theoretical_mean)
  for (j in seq_len(ncol(runs))) {
    x <- runs[, j]
    plot(seq_along(x), x, type = "o", pch = 16, cex = 0.5,
         col = ink, ylim = limits, xlab = "Time index", ylab = "Value",
         main = paste(distribution, "- realization", j))
    abline(h = theoretical_mean, col = accent, lty = 2)
    stats::qqnorm(x, pch = 16, cex = 0.55, col = ink,
                  ylim = limits, main = paste("Normal Q-Q - realization", j),
                  xlab = "Theoretical normal quantile", ylab = "Sample quantile")
    stats::qqline(x, col = accent, lty = 2)
  }
}

run_summary <- function(runs) {
  data.frame(
    Realization = seq_len(ncol(runs)), N = nrow(runs),
    Mean = colMeans(runs), Variance = apply(runs, 2, var),
    Minimum = apply(runs, 2, min), Maximum = apply(runs, 2, max),
    `Lag-1 ACF` = apply(runs, 2, lag1), check.names = FALSE
  )
}
```

## Exercise 3 · Independent normal observations

**Question.** Generate 48 independent normal values, plot them, and repeat. Does the series look random?

We choose $X_t\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1)$, making the unspecified normal parameters explicit. Thus $E(X_t)=0$, $\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=1$, and $\rho(k)=0$ for $k\ne0$.

```{r}
#| label: fig-normal-runs
#| fig-height: 8
#| fig-cap: "Three standard-normal realizations, each of length 48, with normal Q–Q plots. Horizontal dashed lines mark the theoretical mean of zero."
#| fig-alt: "Three rows pairing normal time series with their normal quantile plots; time plots share a common vertical scale."
set.seed(2025)
normal_runs <- replicate(B, stats::rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = 1))
plot_runs(normal_runs, "N(0, 1)", theoretical_mean = 0)
```

```{r}
#| label: tbl-normal-runs
#| tbl-cap: "Sample summaries for the normal realizations; population mean = 0 and variance = 1."
knitr::kable(run_summary(normal_runs), digits = 3)
```

**Answer.** The realizations fluctuate irregularly around zero, and apparent runs or local rises and falls differ between realizations. This appearance is compatible with randomness, but **independence is specified by the simulation model**, not established by the visual impression. A random process need not alternate signs regularly or have sample mean exactly zero.

Across these realizations, sample means range from `r fmt(min(colMeans(normal_runs)), 3)` to `r fmt(max(colMeans(normal_runs)), 3)`, and sample variances range from `r fmt(min(apply(normal_runs, 2, var)), 3)` to `r fmt(max(apply(normal_runs, 2, var)), 3)`. Such differences reflect sampling variation. The standard deviation of a sample mean is $1/\sqrt{48}\approx0.144$, so moderate deviations from zero are expected. Q–Q departures at the extremes of a small sample do not, by themselves, establish that the specified normal generator is incorrect.

## Exercise 4 · Independent chi-square observations

**Question.** Repeat with 48 independent chi-square values with two degrees of freedom. Does it look random and nonnormal?

For $X_t\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\chi^2_2$,

$$
E(X_t)=2,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_t)=4,\qquad
f(x)=\tfrac12 e^{-x/2}\quad(x>0).
$$

This is an exponential distribution with mean 2 and skewness 2. The [R chi-square documentation](https://search.r-project.org/R/refmans/stats/html/Chisquare.html) gives the generator and distribution formulas.

```{r}
#| label: fig-chisq-runs
#| fig-height: 8
#| fig-cap: "Three iid chi-square(2) realizations and normal Q–Q plots. Horizontal dashed lines mark the mean of 2; the raw series has not been centered."
#| fig-alt: "Three nonnegative time series with occasional large positive values paired with normal quantile plots."
set.seed(2026)
chisq_runs <- replicate(B, stats::rchisq(n, df = 2))
plot_runs(chisq_runs, "Chi-square(2)", theoretical_mean = 2)
```

```{r}
#| label: tbl-chisq-runs
#| tbl-cap: "Sample summaries for the chi-square realizations; population mean = 2 and variance = 4."
knitr::kable(run_summary(chisq_runs), digits = 3)
```

**Answer.** The timing of high and low observations is irregular, as expected for iid draws. Nonnegative values and occasional large positive spikes reveal asymmetry more readily than in the normal experiment. The maximum over the three retained paths is `r fmt(max(chisq_runs))`; this is a simulation result, not an upper bound on the distribution. Curvature in the normal Q–Q plots helps distinguish this skewed marginal distribution from a Gaussian one.

These paths can look random **and** nonnormal because the properties concern different aspects of the process. Spikes do not imply serial dependence or data errors. The raw process has a positive mean; $\varepsilon_t=X_t-2$ is zero-mean iid noise with variance 4. Subtracting 2 changes its location but neither its independence nor its skewness. The standard deviation of the sample mean is $2/\sqrt{48}\approx0.289$, twice that of the standard-normal experiment.

## Exercise 5 · Independent Student's t observations

**Question.** Repeat with 48 independent $t$ values with five degrees of freedom. Does it look random and nonnormal?

For $X_t\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}t_5$,

$$
E(X_t)=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\frac{5}{5-2}=\frac53.
$$

The distribution is symmetric with heavier tails than a normal distribution. Its excess kurtosis is $6/(5-4)=6$, compared with zero for a Gaussian distribution. See the [R Student's t documentation](https://search.r-project.org/R/refmans/stats/html/TDist.html) for the generator, density, and mean/variance conditions.

```{r}
#| label: fig-t-runs
#| fig-height: 8
#| fig-cap: "Three iid t(5) realizations and normal Q–Q plots. Horizontal dashed lines mark the theoretical mean of zero."
#| fig-alt: "Three t-distributed time series with normal quantile plots for inspecting their tails."
set.seed(2027)
t_runs <- replicate(B, stats::rt(n, df = 5))
plot_runs(t_runs, "Student t(5)", theoretical_mean = 0)
```

```{r}
#| label: tbl-t-runs
#| tbl-cap: "Sample summaries for the t realizations; population mean = 0 and variance = 5/3."
knitr::kable(run_summary(t_runs), digits = 3)
```

**Answer.** As in the normal experiment, paths fluctuate irregularly around zero. The largest observed absolute value across these paths is `r fmt(max(abs(t_runs)))`. The heavy-tailed model makes large excursions more likely but does not guarantee an obvious extreme in every 48 observations. A short $t_5$ path may therefore resemble a normal path; nonnormality is less visually apparent than for $\chi^2_2$.

When sufficiently extreme observations occur, a normal Q–Q plot tends to depart outward in the tails. At this sample size the pattern can be weak or uneven. We know the simulated population is nonnormal from its generating model, not from whether a particular realization passes a visual or formal normality check.

**A fair comparison of tails.** The raw $t_5$ distribution also has greater variance than $N(0,1)$, so larger raw excursions alone do not isolate tail shape. Set $Z_t=\sqrt{3/5}\,X_t$ to give the $t$ variable unit variance. Compare the probability of exceeding three population standard deviations in absolute value:

```{r}
#| label: tbl-tail-comparison
#| tbl-cap: "Theoretical tail probabilities after matching variance to one; these are not estimates from the 48-point simulations."
tail_probability <- c(
  2 * stats::pnorm(3, lower.tail = FALSE),
  2 * stats::pt(3 * sqrt(5 / 3), df = 5, lower.tail = FALSE)
)
knitr::kable(data.frame(
  Distribution = c("Standard normal", "t(5) scaled by sqrt(3/5)"),
  `P(|Z| > 3)` = tail_probability, check.names = FALSE
), digits = 5)
```

This calculation separates heavier tails from the variance difference. The required time plots remain on their **original scales**; vertical limits are shared within a distribution, not across distributions.

### Supplement · Serial correlation in simulated paths

```{r}
#| label: fig-simulation-acf
#| fig-height: 3.5
#| fig-cap: "ACFs of the first realization from each model, selected in advance rather than for its appearance. Limits are approximate pointwise white-noise references."
#| fig-alt: "Three sample autocorrelation plots for normal, chi-square, and t simulations through lag ten."
local({
  old <- par(mfrow = c(1, 3), mar = c(4, 4, 3, 1))
  on.exit(par(old))
  examples <- list("N(0, 1)" = normal_runs[, 1],
                   "Chi-square(2)" = chisq_runs[, 1],
                   "Student t(5)" = t_runs[, 1])
  for (nm in names(examples)) {
    stats::acf(examples[[nm]], lag.max = 10, ylim = c(-1, 1),
               main = nm, xlab = "Lag (observations)", col = ink)
  }
})
```

The population ACF is zero at every positive lag for all three models, but the **sample** ACF need not be zero. R centers observations when estimating this ACF, including the raw chi-square values. With only 48 observations, the reference limits are approximately $\pm$`r fmt(qnorm(0.975) / sqrt(n), 3)`; isolated crossings can occur by chance, especially when many lags or realizations are inspected. These limits are asymptotic and warrant caution for short, skewed, or heavy-tailed samples. No search for a favorable seed or repeated significance testing is used to certify independence.

## Conclusions

The observed series illustrate annual rainfall variation, persistence in industrial color measurements, and strong annual seasonality in monthly temperatures. The simulations show that independent observations can have Gaussian, skewed, or heavy-tailed distributions. Known population properties must be distinguished from chance features in short realizations. Time plots address temporal structure; Q–Q plots address marginal shape; neither alone proves independence or stationarity.
